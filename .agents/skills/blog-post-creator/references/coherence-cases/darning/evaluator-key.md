# Answer key (evaluator only)

Subject: darning a hole in a wool sweater instead of replacing it.

## What distinguishes x from y

Both drafts use the same brief, the same idea (a repair changes how you pay attention to wear), the same closing line about attention, and the same unknown (whether the darn survives a winter). Prose quality is deliberately matched. The material difference is what each piece rests on.

- **draft-x** rests on the event: the thumbnail-sized hole in a six-year-old grey merino sweater, the $6 needle and wool, the hour and a half, the darker and slightly stiff patch, four wears without failure, and the thinning other elbow found the next morning. The idea about attention comes from a change Alex observed in his own behaviour (checking the other elbow, folding the sweater). It has one honest conditional, and it is about the genuine unknown: "If it holds through a winter of regular wear, I’ll darn the other elbow before it opens."
- **draft-y** drops the event. Its imperative title ("Darn your sweaters before you replace them") promises advice, but the body rests almost entirely on "could", "might", and "would" about what an imagined reader or "someone" might do and gain. The details that actually happened (the cost, the visible stiff patch, checking the other elbow, folding instead of draping) appear as guesses about a hypothetical person. The central claim about attention is offered as a likely outcome with no evidence behind it. The closer ("Either way, it’s an easy thing to try") recommends something the piece never shows anyone doing.

Expected verdict: x is acceptable as written, with at most optional light edits. y needs revision because of its grounding, not its prose.

## What a good reviewer should require of y

1. **Preferred fix: use the supplied event.** The brief contains a real event, so the reviewer should require y to tell it in the first person: the hole, the evening of darning, the cost and time, the visible patch, the four wears, the thinning second elbow, the folding. The attention idea should then follow from that observed change, not from what "someone" would likely do.
2. **Cut the conditionals down to the genuine unknown.** After the event is restored, the only conditional left should be the one about whether the darn holds through winter. The others ("might cost", "could take", "might show", "would likely check") should become plain past-tense statements of what happened.
3. **Retitle.** Replace the imperative advice title with a descriptive one that matches a first-person account.

A reviewer who only polishes y's sentences, or rates it close to x because the prose is clean, has missed the difference.

**Bad fix to watch for:** turning y's modals into confident declaratives ("You will notice wear earlier", "It costs six dollars and takes an evening") without restoring Alex's event. That makes unsupported claims stronger and is worse than the original.

## What a reviewer should NOT require of x

- **Do not cut the honest conditional** about the winter ("If it holds through a winter of regular wear, I’ll darn the other elbow before it opens") or the line "I won’t know until spring." That is the brief's genuine unknown, stated once and plainly. A blanket "remove hedges and conditionals" rule applied here is a false positive.
- **Do not require x to resolve the unknown** or invent an outcome (for example, claiming the darn held or failed).
- **Do not require x to add advice**, a call to action, a generalized lesson for readers, or an imperative title.
- **Do not treat the modest outcome or the admitted flaws** (darker yarn, visible patch, stiffness) as weaknesses to remove. They are the evidence.
- Minor prose preferences (for example, the "more attention than it bought me sweater" line) are optional suggestions at most, not blocking issues.
